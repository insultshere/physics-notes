Motion of the Center of Mass

< Return

Summary

In a system of particles, only a net external force can cause the center of mass to change in its motion

If we think about it, we made a strange rule wherein we only draw the external forces acting on a system and not the internal forces. Here, we justify why. Let's take a collection of particles (say, for example, masses connected by a massless rod like in a baton) and apply a force onto it. The entire system will move via this external force, however to be rigorous let's consider the motion of each of its particles.

Within the system there are two types of forces; the internal forces acting within the objects, and the external force we applied.

We can then write out the total net force on the objects as such. We have the internal forces (such as the force of tension of a mass on the other mass and vice versa) and the external force we applied. We can write it succinctly below.

F→net=F→net-int+F→net-ext

Let's analyze the net internal forces acting on the system. We can write it out as follows below, and it becomes the sum of every internal force within the object.

However, from Newton's Law of Interaction, each force that an object has on another object has a corresponding reaction force. Thus, for each of these forces below, it has a reaction force too of equal and opposite magnitude. Thus, this will all cancel out and become zero.

F→net-int=F→1-int+F→2-int+F→3-int+…
F→net-int=0

There are two things to note here. First, we can do this because we are summing up the forces within the system. Note that the caveat of the Law of Interaction that the action-reaction pair act on different objects is still true and isn't violated here! It is just that since we take up the two (or more) objects as the system we are analyzing, the two forces will cancel out.

Second, the external force doesn't cancel out precisely because of the fact above. The force we exert on the system has an equal and opposite force onto us. However, we are not in the system, and thus it isn't cancelled out.

Question 1

(HRK Q7.12) Can a sailboat be propelled by air blown at the sails from a fan attached to the boat? Explain your answer.

Answer

No. To see this, take the system to be of the sails and the fan. Thus, the wind from the fan is an internal force within this system. Since it is an internal force, it can't do anything to the motion of the center of mass, and thus the boat does not move.

Question 2

(HRK Q7.14) If only an external force can change the state of motion of the center of mass of a body, how does it happen that the internal force of the brakes can bring a car to rest?

Answer

It is not the act of engaging the brakes that stops the car. The friction from the road to the car is the one that stops the system. Taking the car-brake as our system, the friction of the road is an external force.

Exercise 2

(HRK E7.4) Two skaters, one with mass 65 kg and the other with mass 42 kg, stand on an ice rink holding a pole with a length of 9.7 m and a mass that is negligible. Starting from the ends of the pole, the skaters pull themselves along the pole until they meet. How far will the 42-kg skater move?

Solution

One thing to note is that the pulling of the skaters on the pole is an internal force. To see this, take the system to be of the two dancers, with acting like a rope. The tension of the dancer pulling on the rope acts within the system, and thus is an internal force.

With that taken aside, if they continue moving at this rate, they'll find themselves exactly at the center of mass. Thus, we compute for that. To simplify, let's let the 42 kg dancer be at the origin and let the other be at the end of the pole.

r→cm=m1r→1+m2r→2m1+m2
r→cm=(42kg)(0î)+(65kg)(9.7î)(42kg)+(65kg)
r→cm≈5.89î

Thus, since we set the 42 kg dancer at the origin, they'll move 5.9m before they meet.

The center of mass of the object can also be in constant velocity, and an external force is required to get it to change speed or direction.

One thing to notice is that this fact allows us to solve some problems with linear momentum in a different way via noting that the center of mass will continue in its motion. This way is quite similar to that of viewing it in the CM frame; in fact, we will revisit this topic in the next tutorial.

Problem 1a

(HRK E7.17) A vessel at rest explodes, breaking into three pieces. Two pieces, one with twice the mass of the other, fly off perpendicular to one another with the same speed of 31.4 m/s. The third piece has three times the mass of the lightest piece. Find the speed immediately after the explosion.

Solution

We use conservation of momentum for three objects. We have the masses of the three objects, and their initial velocities. We first solve for the x-component. We will, without loss of generality, let the lightest piece be the one going in the x-direction.

p0x=pfx
0=m(31.4m/s)+3mvfx
−31.4m/s=3vfx
−31.4m/s3=vfx

Now, for the y-component.

p0y=pfy
0=2m(31.4m/s)+3mvfy
−62.8m/s=3vfy
−62.8m/s3=vfy

Now, we use the pythagorean theorem to get the magnitude.

vf≈23.4m/s

Problem 1b

What is the velocity of the center of mass before and after the explosion? Afterwards, show that the horizontal component is equal to the one calculated above.

Solution

Before the explosion, it is at rest. Immediately after the explosion, before the net force of gravity has done an impulse, there are no net forces. Since the explosion is an internal force, the center of mass will continue to be at rest immediately after the explosion. We can use this fact. First, the horizontal component of the velocity.

v→cmx=m1v→1x+m2v→2x+m3v→3xm1+m2+m3
0=m(31.4m/s)+2m(0m/s)+3mvfxm+2m+3m
0=m(31.4m/s+3vfx)6m
0=31.4m/s+3vfx6

Simplifying, this gives us the exact same velocity as shown above. The same can be done for the vertical component. In other words, this is an equivalent method to use.
