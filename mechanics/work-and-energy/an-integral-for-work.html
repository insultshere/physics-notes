An Integral for Work

< Return

Summary

Work is the integral of force over a displacement with respect to position

Equations

∫x0xFdx=W

We will now generalize our findings in the last tutorial to deal with any function of force over a distance. We saw that work is the area under the force-position graph; thus, it corresponds to its definite integral.

First, note that the displacement, which here we will write as Δx is the same as the difference of the initial position and the final position, shown below.

Δt=(xf−x0)

Graphically, this corresponds to the starting point of our integral and the ending point of our integral.

Notice how here the force can be negative. This just means that force can sometimes be pointing opposite the direction of displacement.

This also gives us the bounds for our integral. Thus, we get that work is the integral of force with respect to time from the initial to the final position.

Specifically, the integral gives us the sum of the signed area of the graph; negative force gives us negative area while positive force gives us positive area. This makes sense; if a force is negative, or points away from the displacement, it does negative work.

Exercise 1a

A force varies based on Fs=−2x. What is the work done by the force in displacing an object 5.00 meters from its initial position?

Such a force is used by a spring. The negative sign is used such that the force goes opposite the displacement, allowing it to return to its initial position; the coefficient is used to determine the "springiness" of the spring.

Solution

We are given the force, so we will just use the integral.

∫0.005.00Fsdx=W
∫0.005.00−2xdx=W
−2∫0.005.00xdx=W
−2x22|0.005.00=W
−2(5)22=−6.25J=W

This exercise gives us a unique type of force that we will analyze throughout this topic. We will give it a nickname, calling it the "spring force". Of course, here, we won't deal too much with its dynamics, but just how it plays with work.

Here, we will let the force be a more general form, Fs=−kx. The reasoning for this formation is said in the above exercise; the k is the coefficient said, which gives the "springiness" of the spring. The higher this constant, the more the stiff the spring is.

As seen, the graph forms a triangle with base d and height −kd, which if we use the description of work such that it is the area under the graph, will give the same as above.

Exercise 1b

Compute for the work done by the spring force, Fs=−kx, displaced a distance d from its initial position. Let k be a positive constant.

Solution

We will use the integral method first.

∫0d−kxdx=W
−k∫0dxdx=W
−kx22|0d=W
−kd22=−12kd2=W

An alternate approach is just to use the area definition. It is, as said, a triangle.

W=12bh
W=−12dkd
W=−12kd2

Exercise 2

(HRK P11.13) A "stiff" spring has a force law given by Fs=−kx3. The work required to stretch the spring a distance d from the initial position is W0. In terms of W0, how much work is required to extend the spring from d to the length 2d?

Solution

First, let's see what W0 is in terms of d. Setting up the integral, we have the following.

∫0d−kx3dx=W0
−k∫0dx3dx=W0
−kx44|0d=W0
−kd44=−14kd4=W0

Now, we change the bounds of integration to see how much is needed to stretch it to 2d.

∫d2d−kx3dx=W
−k∫d2dx3dx=W
−kx44|d2d=W
−k[(2d)44−d44]=W
−k[16d44−d44]=W
−k15d44=W

If we factor out the 15, we can see that it is exactly such that of W0.

15(−14kd4)=W
15W0=W

We end this formally, like we did with the integral for impulse. We once again divide the graph into tiny portions of δx, or small positions of the displacement.

Thus, we have cut up the graph into tiny rectangles, each with a tiny area δW. This area is given by the product of the length and height of the rectangle. The length is set as δx, and the height is given by the force at that position δx.

We can then add up all of these tiny δW to get the total work.

W=∑δW
W=∑F(δx)δx

Now, this is just an approximation. However, as the small parts δW approach zero, the tiny rectangles will perfectly encapsulate the area of the entire graph. Thus, we rewrite the sum into an integral.

W=∫dW
W=∫Fdx
