Linear Mass Density

< Return

Summary

The linear mass density of a rigid object is the mass distributed to each infinitesimal length

Equations

λdx=dm
M=∫0Lλdx

So far, we have used the following relation to simplify our work. We've used it in thin rods were we set the mass to be evenly distributed along the entire rod.

ML=dmdx
MLdx=dm

The left side of the equation has a special name. It is called the linear mass density. Intuitively, it is how the mass is distributed over the length of the entire rod.

Linear Mass Density (λ) - the mass per unit length of a thin rod

In a rod with uniform linear mass density, like the ones we've tackled, it is as if each tiny infinitesimal length has the same cut of the entire mass; in other words, it is evenly distributed. If we use this symbol, we can write out our relation as the following below.

MLdx=dm
λdx=dm

This relation may now be useful for thin rods with non-linear mass density. Such rods can occur because of various compounds inside, which can end up on one side.

In such rods, it may be that the total mass is not given. For example, let's take for example a rod with a non uniform mass density. Rewriting, we can get the total mass by integrating.

λdx=dm
∫0Lλdx=∫0Mdm0
∫0Lλdx=M

The above physically means taking the mass of each of the tiny slices of the rod and adding them all up, which intuitively gives the whole mass.

Exercise 1

A thin rod of length L has a constant mass uniformly distributed, with its linear mass density λ being constant. What is its mass?

Solution

We use the above equation. Since the linear mass density is constant, we can take it out.

∫0Lλdx=M
λ∫0Ldx=M
λL=M

This makes sense. If we rearrange it, we get the expression above.

λ=ML

Exercise 2a

A thin rod of length L with non-uniform mass density given by λ(x)=cx is placed on the x-axis on the origin. What is the mass of the rod? Express your answer in terms of the constant c and the length of the rod.

Solution

We substitute in the function above, noting for a small slice dx on the x-axis it will be λ(L)=cL.

∫0Lλdx=M
∫0Lcxdx=M
c∫0Lxdx=M

All we need to do now is integrate that.

cx22|0L=M
cL22=M

Problem 1

(LSM P9.69) Find the center of mass of a rod of length L whose mass density changes from one end to the other quadratically. That is, if the rod is laid out along the x-axis with one end at the origin and the other end at x=L, the density is given by the following equation:

λ(x)=c0+(cf−c0)(xL)2

Where c0 and cf are constant values.

Solution

The first thing to find is the total mass of the rod.

∫0Lλdx=M
∫0L(c0+(cf−c0)(xL)2)dx=M
∫0Lc0dx+∫0L(cf−c0)(xL)2dx=M
c0∫0Ldx+(cf−c0)L2∫0Lx2dx=M
c01|0L+(cf−c0)L2x33|0L=M
c0L+L(cf−c0)3=M
Lcf+2Lc03=M

Alright, now we have to do all that again but this time for the center of mass.

xcm=1M∫xdm
xcm=1M∫xλdx

We note that the integral on the right is basically the one at the top but with an extra x.

xcm=1M(∫0Lc0xdx+∫0L(cf−c0)(xL)2xdx)
xcm=1M(c0∫0Lxdx+(cf−c0)L2∫0Lx3dx)
xcm=1M(c0x22|0L+(cf−c0)L2x44|0L)
xcm=1M(c0L22+L2(cf−c0)4)
xcm=1M(L2cf+L2c04)

Okay, now we will substitute in the mass.

xcm=1Lcf+2Lc03(L2cf+L2c04)
xcm=3Lcf+2Lc0(L2cf+L2c04)

This seems horrific to simplify. However, it is actually quite simple. If we factor it correctly, it simplifies quite nicely.

xcm=3L1cf+2c0·L24cf+c01
xcm=3L4cf+c0cf+2c0

The above is the final answer.

Problem 2

(HRK P7.3) A uniform flexible chain of length L, with weight per unit length λ, passes over a small, frictionless peg. It is released from a rest position with a length of chain x hanging from one side and a length L−x from the other side. Find the acceleration of the system as a function of x.

Solution

Instead of dealing with the whole chain, we just deal with it like it is two masses on each side of the pulley. In other words, it is an Atwood's machine.

Since we are given the weight per unit length, we can get the mass of each side. We will assume that L<2x, or that the side with x length is more massive.

M=λx
m=λ(L−x)

Now, let's recall the acceleration we got for an Atwood's machine.

g(M−mM+m)=a
g(λx−λ(L−x)λx+λ(L−x))=a
g(x−L+xx+L−x)=a
g(2x−LL)=a

If you have been following, you may have noticed that for the past problems that we have written the following integral.

xcm=1M∫xλdx

We can use integration by parts here, instead of having to distribute. This may save some time, however the latter method still works quite well.

Problem 3

Show that for a thin rod of length L, with one end at the origin and placed on the x-axis, when the linear mass density varies as a function of x, that the following holds true.

xcm=L−1M∫0Lmdx

Here, m represents the following integral, which can be taken as the cumulative mass from 0 to x.

∫λdx=m

Solution

We will integrate from 0 to L. We use integration by parts to split the integral.

xcm=1M∫0Lxλdx
xcm=1M(∫0L1dx∫0Lλdx−∫0L(∫λdx)dx)

This ugly expression makes it seem like we did this for nothing. However, we note the following two relations we found.

∫λdx=m
∫0Lλdx=M

This is exactly what is above! Thus, substituting in, we have the following equation.

xcm=1M(LM−∫0Lmdx)
xcm=L−1M∫0Lmdx
