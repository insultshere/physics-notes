Summary

In the CM frame, a collision appears to be head-on with zero initial and final momentum

The speed of the CM frame is constant before and after collision

We noted before that we can do physics in any reference frame. So far, we have analyzed collisions all in a stationary reference frame. For example, an elastic collision is shown below.

This reference frame is called the "lab frame".

Lab Frame - a reference frame where the observer is stationary with respect to the ground

Obviously, it is completely alright to use this reference frame. In fact, it has served as well for the past few tutorials. However, in this tutorial, we will leave this reference frame and try another one, which may be more useful.

One useful property we can do is setting the initial momentum to zero. If this is true, it simplifies our calculations a lot. So much so, that we have a special name for this reference frame.

CM Frame - a reference frame wherein the initial momentum of the system appears to be zero

Below is a visual interpretation of what this frame would look like. Here, we can see that the two objects appear to have the same magnitude of momentum, but in opposite directions; meaning their initial momenta is zero.

We can analyze the two reference frames to see they have the same visual cues; at the stationary reference frame, if you focus at the top, the objects do appear to collide head on.

Meanwhile, in the moving reference frame, it can be seen that before collision, the black object is half in front of the object, while the red object is in front after collision. The same phenomena appears.

Question 1

In the CM frame, the initial momenta of the objects is zero. Below shows a collision in this CM frame, with the right object having more mass than the other object.

In this reference frame, are the object's speeds equal? If not, which of the two speeds is larger?

Answer

We first write out the equation for the initial momentum, which should be zero.

p0′=pf′
p0′=0

Now, we can write out the equation for the initial momentum in terms of the masses and speeds.

mvm0′+MvM0′=0
mvm0′=−MvM0′

Since M>m, it must mean that the speeds must not be equal for this equality to hold. Further, this means the speed of the more massive object is less than the less massive object.

To begin on how we can find this frame, we will first use its defining factor, which is the initial momentum is zero.

p0′=pf′
p0′=0

To have this happen, we need to have the speed of the moving reference frame. Using our equation for relative motion, we have two equations for the speed. We will use our old syntax, with objects of masses m and M. We denote the speed of the reference frame with vcm.

vm0=vm0′+vcm
vM0=vM0′+vcm

Now, let's write the equation for the conservation of momentum in the CM frame, and then substitute in the values above.

p0′=0
mvm0′+MvM0′=0
m(vm0−vcm)+M(vM0−vcm)=0

Now, we can solve for the speed of the reference frame.

mvm0−mvcm+MvM0−Mvcm=0
mvm0+MvM0=mvcm+Mvcm
mvm0+MvM0=vcm(m+M)
vcm=mvm0+MvM0m+M

Thus, if we move at this speed, it will appear that the initial momentum is zero. Consequently, by the conservation of momentum, it means that the final momentum after collision must also be zero.

It may help to notice that the "CM" frame is precisely the center-of-mass of a system. In other words, if we use the reference frame of the center of mass, it will appear as if the initial momenta of the system is zero.

Further, the equation we derived is precisely the same as the one we derived last time.

Question 2

You are on a firework date, and see the fireworks explode, like below. Your friend tells you that the center of mass of the firework, even after exploding, continues to move in a parabolic arc. How do you respond?

Answer

After the explosion, the only net force acting on the system is gravity. Thus, the net force acting on the center of mass is gravity, and thus it is in free fall. If it was projected at an angle, it means it continues to trace a parabolic arc.

The reason why the explosion doesn't change the motion of the CM is because it is an internal force.

Question 3

Show that the speed of the CM frame is constant before and after a head-on collision.

Answer

We first write out the equation for the speed of the CM frame.

vcm=mvm0+MvM0m+M

Notice how the above is precisely the initial momentum of the two objects with respect to the lab frame. Thus, we can rewrite the equation like so.

vcm=p0m+M

Since momentum is conserved, this must be equal to the momentum of the objects with respect to the lab frame after collision; thus, they must be equal.

vcm=pfm+M

Here, we derived the equation for the speed of the CM frame for two particles. However, using the formula we got for multiple particles, we can extend this fact to deal with collisions involving multiple particles.

Sadly, unlike for two particle collisions, there is no set equation for the final velocity of any of the particles given the initial momenta, even if we use the CM frame. Nevertheless, the CM frame can prove useful to simplify these situations.

Problem 1

(HRK E7.18) A railway flat car is rushing along a level frictionless track at a speed of 45 m/s. Mounted on the car and aimed forward is a cannon that fires 65-kg cannon balls with a muzzle speed of 625 m/s. The total mass of the car, the cannon, and the large supply of cannon balls on the car is 3500 kg. How many cannon balls must be fired to bring the car as close to rest as possible?

Solution

We take the entire system to be the car, the cannon, and the cannonballs, even when they are in the air. Now, we can first treat the entire system as a particle, and thus the CM of that system moves at the same speed as the particle, or 45m/s.

vcm=p0m+M
vcm=Mv0M=v0

With this system, we need not care about how the cannonball is shot out since that is an internal force. Now, let's let N be the number of cannonballs thrown out, and M be the total mass of the system.

Now, since there are no external forces, the velocity of the center of mass must still be 45 m/s. Here, we write out at the left the momentum of the cannonballs and on the right the momentum of the car, which is zero since we want it at rest.

vcm=pfm+M
vcm=Nmvbf+(M−Nm)vcfM=v0
NmvbfM=v0
N=Mv0mvbf=(3500kg)(45m/s)(65kg)(625m/s)≈3.88

Thus, four cannonballs need to be shot out.

One thing to note about the above two problems is that we take a system wherein mass is constant. In this system, momentum is conserved, and we can waive the internal forces, and thus the center of mass of the system does not move.
